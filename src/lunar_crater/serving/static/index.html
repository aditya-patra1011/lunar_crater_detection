The problem

Why find craters automatically?

Crater maps are used to pick safe landing sites, to help spacecraft work out where they are, and to estimate how old a surface is: older ground has collected more craters. Today's best catalogue was made largely by hand, by tracing rims on images, and that does not scale to new regions or sharper data.

What the model gets

A square of terrain about 30 km across, as a grid of 256 × 256 heights, one every 118 m. Nothing else: no photos, no hints about where craters are.

  • Craters show up as bowls with a raised rim.
  • Old craters are worn down and faint; new ones are sharp.
  • The smallest counted craters (1 km) are only about 8 pixels wide.

What it must return

Every crater it can find: its centre and radius in the tile, its latitude, longitude and diameter on the Moon, and a confidence score.

  • Answers are checked against the Robbins catalogue of 1.3 million lunar craters.
  • That catalogue is incomplete for the smallest craters, so a "wrong" detection may be a real crater nobody listed.
Our approach

How it was built and tested

Each step was fixed before the next was tried, and the final scores come from test regions that no model or setting was ever tuned on.

1

Cut the Moon into tiles

3,000 tiles at random places, each reprojected so craters stay round even near the poles, with the catalogue's craters drawn onto each one.

2

Keep a fair test

Tiles were split by region, not at random: 2,071 tiles to learn from, 450 to tune on and 429 to test on, from separate parts of the Moon.

3

Build three detectors

A hand-written slope filter as a baseline, then a neural network (U-Net) that learns what rims look like, then a second network just for large craters.

4

Score them the same way

A detection counts as correct only if its circle overlaps a catalogue crater by at least half (IoU of 0.5 or more), each crater matched once.

The result

How well it works on unseen terrain

All scores below are on the 429 test tiles. F1 combines two questions into one number from 0 to 1: how many real craters did it find, and how many of its answers were right? A detector that finds everything but raises many false alarms scores low, and so does one that is always right but finds little.

Learning beats hand-written rules

The U-Net lifts the overall score from 0.37 to 0.53 and makes far fewer false alarms: 72% of its answers match the catalogue, against 35%.

2

A second network fixed large craters

The U-Net alone stitched arcs of small craters into fake large ones. A network trained only on craters of 6 km and wider raised the score over 8 km from 0.35 to 0.56.

3

Small craters are the hard part

Craters of 1 to 2 km are 70% of all test craters, and only 38% of them are found. This is now the biggest gap.

4

The gains are real, not luck

Re-scoring on random re-draws of the 7 test regions, the improvement over the baseline stays between +0.10 and +0.25.

Limits and next steps

What to keep in mind

Limits

  • It finds fewer than half of catalogued craters, and only 38% of the 1–2 km ones.
  • Craters wider than about 32 km are not found; they rarely fit inside one tile.
  • Tiles must be 256 × 256 heights, 118 m apart, prepared like the training tiles. The service checks the shape but cannot check the scale.
  • The test covers 7 regions of the Moon; results on other terrain may differ.
  • It is a research and portfolio project, not checked for landing or navigation decisions.

Still to do, and later

  • Watch for incoming tiles that differ from the training data (drift monitoring).
  • Turn scores into plain probabilities a reader can trust.
  • Check a sample of "not in catalogue" detections by eye to see how many are real craters.
  • Later: better recall on small craters, craters over 32 km, and an object-detection model.
